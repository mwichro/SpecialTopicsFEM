# Lecture 19: Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) for the Laplace Problem

**Goal:** Formulate the Symmetric Interior Penalty Galerkin (SIPG) method for the Poisson problem ($-\Delta u = f$) and rigorously prove that it is consistent and well-posed.

 The strategy is to establish the algebraic tools (averages and jumps) first, derive the method physically, and then dedicate the second half to the functional analysis (norms, trace inequalities, and coercivity) that proves why the method actually works.

## 1. The Broken Space and DG Operators

In standard FEM, we demand our functions be globally continuous ($H^1(\Omega)$). In DG, we relax this. Functions are polynomials inside each element, but can be completely discontinuous across element faces.

**1. The Broken Space ($V_h$):**
Let $\mathcal{T}_h$ be our mesh. We define the "broken" finite element space:
$$ V_h = \{ v \in L^2(\Omega) : v|_K \in \mathbb{P}^k(K) \quad \forall K \in \mathcal{T}_h \} $$
*Note: Because $V_h \not\subset H^1(\Omega)$, standard integration by parts over the whole domain $\Omega$ is illegal! We must integrate element-by-element.*

**2. Faces, Averages, and Jumps:**
Let $\mathcal{E}_h$ be the set of all faces (edges in 2D). For an interior face $e$ shared by elements $K^+$ and $K^-$ with outward normals $n^+$ and $n^-$, we define two operators for a scalar $v$ and a vector $\boldsymbol{\tau}$:
*   **Average $\{\cdot\}$:** 
    $$ \{v\} = \frac{1}{2}(v^+ + v^-), \qquad \{\boldsymbol{\tau}\} = \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^+ + \boldsymbol{\tau}^-) $$
*   **Jump $\llbracket \cdot \rrbracket$:** (Scalar jump is a vector; vector jump is a scalar!)
    $$ \llbracket v \rrbracket = v^+ n^+ + v^- n^-, \qquad \llbracket \boldsymbol{\tau} \rrbracket = \boldsymbol{\tau}^+ \cdot n^+ + \boldsymbol{\tau}^- \cdot n^- $$
*(On Dirichlet boundaries $\partial \Omega$, we define $\{v\} = v$, $\llbracket v \rrbracket = v n$, $\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\} = \boldsymbol{\tau}$, $\llbracket \boldsymbol{\tau} \rrbracket = \boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot n$).*

---

## 2. Derivation of the SIPG Formulation

We want to solve $-\Delta u = f$ with $u=0$ on $\partial \Omega$. 

**Step 1: Element-wise Integration by Parts**
Multiply by a discontinuous test function $v \in V_h$ and integrate over a *single* element $K$:
$$ -\int_K (\Delta u) v \, dx = \int_K \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \, dx - \int_{\partial K} (\nabla u \cdot n_K) v \, ds = \int_K f v \, dx $$

**Step 2: Sum over all elements**
Sum the equation over all $K \in \mathcal{T}_h$. The volume integrals simply sum up. The boundary integrals sum over all element boundaries, which we regroup into a sum over the unique faces $e \in \mathcal{E}_h$. 
*(Write this DG Magic Identity on the board):*
$$ \sum_K \int_{\partial K} (\boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot n_K) v \, ds = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_h} \int_e \left( \{\boldsymbol{\tau}\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket + \llbracket \boldsymbol{\tau} \rrbracket \{v\} \right) ds $$

Applying this to $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \nabla u$, we get:
$$ \sum_K \int_K \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \, dx - \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_h} \int_e \left( \{\nabla u\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket + \llbracket \nabla u \rrbracket \{v\} \right) ds = \int_\Omega f v \, dx $$

**Step 3: Enforcing Physics (Smoothness of exact solution)**
Because the exact solution $u$ is smooth (no kinks), its flux is continuous across faces. Therefore, the jump of its gradient is strictly zero: $\llbracket \nabla u \rrbracket = 0$. The second face integral vanishes!
$$ \sum_K \int_K \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \, dx - \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_h} \int_e \{\nabla u\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket \, ds = \int_\Omega f v \, dx $$

**Step 4: Symmetrization and Penalty (The SIPG Form)**
The current bilinear form is not symmetric. Furthermore, it doesn't force the discontinuous pieces of $v$ to hold together. We add two terms that are mathematically identically zero for the exact solution $u$ (since $\llbracket u \rrbracket = 0$):
1.  **Symmetry term:** $-\int_e \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \{\nabla v\} \, ds$
2.  **Interior Penalty term:** $+\frac{\sigma}{h_e} \int_e \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket \, ds$

This yields the **Symmetric Interior Penalty Galerkin (SIPG)** bilinear form:
$$ a_h(u, v) = \sum_K \int_K \nabla u \cdot \nabla v - \sum_e \int_e \left( \{\nabla u\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket + \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \{\nabla v\} \right) + \sum_e \frac{\sigma}{h_e} \int_e \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket $$

---

## 3. The DG Energy Norm and Trace Inequalities (Tools for Proofs)

To prove this works, we must define the metric of our broken space. 
**Definition (DG Energy Norm):**
$$ |||v|||^2 := \sum_{K} \|\nabla v\|_{L^2(K)}^2 + \sum_{e} \frac{\sigma}{h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_{L^2(e)}^2 $$
*(Notice it combines the volume kinetic energy with the face-jump penalty energy).*

To relate volume integrals to face integrals during our proofs, we need a mathematical tool.
**Lemma (Discrete Trace/Inverse Inequality):**
For polynomial functions $v \in \mathbb{P}^k(K)$, the value of the function on the boundary of the element is strictly bounded by its value inside the element, scaled by the element size $h_K$:
$$ \|v\|_{L^2(\partial K)}^2 \le C_{inv} h_K^{-1} \|v\|_{L^2(K)}^2 $$
Consequently, applying this to the gradient $\nabla v$:
$$ h_K \|\nabla v\|_{L^2(\partial K)}^2 \le C_{inv} \|\nabla v\|_{L^2(K)}^2 $$
*(Leave this on a side-board. We will desperately need it for coercivity!)*

---

## 4. Proof 1: Consistency (Exactness)

A numerical scheme is *consistent* if the exact solution $u$ of the PDE exactly satisfies the discrete variational formulation: $a_h(u, v) = L(v) \;\forall v \in V_h$.

**Proof:**
Substitute the exact solution $u$ into the SIPG bilinear form.
Because $u \in H^2(\Omega)$, it is continuous across all faces. Therefore, its jump is zero: $\llbracket u \rrbracket = 0$.
Look at the bilinear form $a_h(u, v)$:
*   The symmetry term vanishes: $\int_e \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \{\nabla v\} = 0$.
*   The penalty term vanishes: $\frac{\sigma}{h_e} \int_e \llbracket u \rrbracket \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket = 0$.

We are left exactly with the formulation we derived at the end of Step 3:
$$ a_h(u, v) = \sum_K \int_K \nabla u \cdot \nabla v - \sum_e \int_e \{\nabla u\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket = \int_\Omega f v = L(v) $$
The scheme is completely consistent. $\blacksquare$

*(Corollary: Galerkin Orthogonality. $a_h(u - u_h, v) = 0$. The error is orthogonal to the discrete space!)*

---

## 5. Proof 2: Well-Posedness (Coercivity)

*This is the core of the lecture. We must prove that $a_h(v, v) \ge \alpha |||v|||^2$ to use the Lax-Milgram theorem. This proof explains exactly why we need the penalty parameter $\sigma$.*

**Step 1: Write out $a_h(v, v)$**
$$ a_h(v, v) = \underbrace{\sum_K \|\nabla v\|_K^2}_{\text{Volume}} - \underbrace{2 \sum_e \int_e \{\nabla v\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket}_{\text{Cross Term}} + \underbrace{\sum_e \frac{\sigma}{h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e^2}_{\text{Penalty}} $$
We have a positive volume term, a positive penalty term, but a dangerous negative cross-term. We must prove that the penalty term is strong enough to "absorb" the cross term!

**Step 2: Bound the Cross Term (Young's Inequality)**
Recall the $\epsilon$-Young Inequality: $2ab \le \epsilon a^2 + \frac{1}{\epsilon} b^2$ for any $\epsilon > 0$.
Let's bound the face integral. We creatively insert $h_e^{1/2}$ and $h_e^{-1/2}$:
$$ 2 \int_e \{\nabla v\} \cdot \llbracket v \rrbracket \le 2 \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e = 2 \left( h_e^{1/2} \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e \right) \left( h_e^{-1/2} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e \right) $$
Apply Young's Inequality:
$$ \le \epsilon h_e \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e^2 + \frac{1}{\epsilon h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e^2 $$

**Step 3: Invoke the Trace Inequality**
We have a term $h_e \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e^2$. We use our discrete trace inequality to push this from the face $e$ back into the adjacent elements $K^+$ and $K^-$:
$$ h_e \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e^2 \le C_{inv} \left( \|\nabla v\|_{K^+}^2 + \|\nabla v\|_{K^-}^2 \right) $$
Summing this over all faces gives:
$$ \sum_e \epsilon h_e \| \{\nabla v\} \|_e^2 \le \epsilon C_{inv} \sum_K \|\nabla v\|_{K}^2 $$

**Step 4: Combine the Bounds**
Substitute our bound for the cross-term back into the original $a_h(v,v)$ equation:
$$ a_h(v, v) \ge \sum_K \|\nabla v\|_K^2 - \left( \epsilon C_{inv} \sum_K \|\nabla v\|_{K}^2 + \sum_e \frac{1}{\epsilon h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e^2 \right) + \sum_e \frac{\sigma}{h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e^2 $$

Factor out the volume terms and the jump terms:
$$ a_h(v, v) \ge \underbrace{(1 - \epsilon C_{inv})}_{C_1} \sum_K \|\nabla v\|_K^2 + \underbrace{\left(\sigma - \frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)}_{C_2} \sum_e \frac{1}{h_e} \|\llbracket v \rrbracket\|_e^2 $$

**Step 5: The "Aha!" Moment (Choosing $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$)**
For coercivity, we *must* have $C_1 > 0$ and $C_2 > 0$. 
1.  To make $C_1 > 0$, we must pick a small $\epsilon$. Let's choose $\epsilon = \frac{1}{2 C_{inv}}$. 
    Then $C_1 = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$.
2.  Substitute $\epsilon$ into $C_2$: 
    $$ C_2 = \sigma - 2 C_{inv} $$
    For $C_2 > 0$, we **strictly require** the penalty parameter $\sigma > 2 C_{inv}$.

*(Point to the board)*: This is why DG methods have a penalty parameter! If $\sigma$ is too small, the cross-term destroys the positivity of the operator, and the matrix becomes indefinite. The penalty must be large enough to overcome the inverse trace constant of the polynomials!

If we choose $\sigma$ sufficiently large, we define $\alpha = \min( \frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sigma - 2C_{inv}}{\sigma} ) > 0$, and we get:
$$ a_h(v, v) \ge \alpha |||v|||^2 \quad \blacksquare $$

*(Note for completeness: Continuity $a_h(u,v) \le M |||u||| \, |||v|||$ is proven similarly by just applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the three terms in the bilinear form, and using the trace inequality on the symmetry term. Lax-Milgram is perfectly satisfied, and the discrete problem is uniquely solvable!)*
